Welcome

The objective of this course is to become familiar with the equipment and techniques of experimental physics: radiation detectors and their signals, electronic signal processing (timing and energy), data acquisition, statistical analysis and curve fitting, and the communication of results in the forms working physicists actually use, journal-style reports, posters, and talks. Most of the experiments in this lab replicate breakthrough measurements that earned (or famously deserved) Nobel Prizes; you will reproduce them with research-grade equipment.

Energy spectroscopy

Alpha Spectroscopy

When a charged particle plows through matter, it loses energy in thousands of tiny collisions with electrons, and the rate of that loss, the stopping power, is one of the most consequential quantities in applied physics. It decides how thick a spacecraft’s shielding must be, how deep a proton-therapy beam penetrates before depositing its dose in a tumor, and how a particle detector tells one particle from another. In this experiment you send alpha particles through metal foils inside a vacuum chamber and watch their energy drain away, turning a century-old piece of physics (first worked out by Bohr and Bethe) into numbers you measured yourself. The sharp rise in energy loss just before a charged particle stops, the Bragg peak, carries the name of William Henry Bragg, who mapped alpha stopping in the 1900s, and it is the reason charged-particle therapy works: a proton beam can be tuned to dump its dose at a tumor’s depth and almost nowhere else. The newest frontier takes your exact subject to its extreme: targeted alpha therapy attaches alpha emitters to tumor-seeking molecules, and because an alpha’s stopping power is so enormous, its entire lethal dose lands within a few cell diameters of where it decays, killing the cancer cell it’s docked to while sparing the neighborhood.

The physics has a satisfying why to it: a slow particle lingers near each electron it passes, so the slower an alpha gets, the harder it brakes (stopping power grows roughly as 1/v²), until it dumps everything at once and stops. That is the whole secret of the Bragg peak. And the same signature, how hard a particle brakes versus how fast it moves, is how modern detectors from collider experiments to space instruments tell one particle species from another, which makes this lab a first lesson in particle identification.

What you will measure: the energy spectrum of an alpha source, and the energy lost in foils of different metals and thicknesses, giving you the stopping power dE/dx of each material and a comparison against theory.

Gamma Spectroscopy

Every radioactive nucleus broadcasts its identity as a fingerprint of gamma-ray energies, and reading that fingerprint is how we monitor reactors, screen cargo for smuggled nuclear material, date archaeological finds, and identify newly synthesized elements. The instrument of choice is the high-purity germanium (HPGe) detector, a single crystal of the purest material humans know how to make, whose energy resolution is sharp enough to split lines that other detectors blur together. That sharpness also opens a window into nuclear structure: some nuclei rotate like tiny quantum tops, and their spectra form rotational bands whose spacing reveals the shape and moment of inertia of the nucleus itself. And the moment of inertia is where it gets genuinely strange, because measuring it amounts to asking: when a nucleus spins, what kind of object is it? A rigid body gives one answer, a drop of ordinary fluid gives another, and the measured value refuses both, landing stubbornly in between, because the paired nucleons form something like a superfluid that only partially follows the rotation. Your measurement catches a 166-nucleon quantum superfluid in the act of being neither solid nor liquid; explaining that weirdness earned Aage Bohr, Ben Mottelson, and James Rainwater the 1975 Nobel Prize.

What you will measure: calibrated gamma-ray spectra from known and unknown sources, the energy resolution of your detector, and the rotational band of a deformed nucleus, from which you extract its moment of inertia and test it against the rigid-body and fluid pictures, expecting it to match neither.

Rutherford Scattering

In 1911 this experiment rewrote the atom. The picture going in was J.J. Thomson’s “plum pudding”: electrons studded through a diffuse ball of positive charge, the atom’s mass and charge spread smoothly, nothing hard anywhere inside. In that atom an alpha particle has nothing to hit, so it should shoulder straight through a gold foil’s thousands of atomic layers, nudged by tiny fractions of a degree, and even the accumulated wobble of all those nudges predicts essentially nothing at large angles. That is what Rutherford, already a Nobel laureate for his radioactivity work, expected when he set Hans Geiger and a twenty-year-old undergraduate, Ernest Marsden, to watch for scattered alphas. Instead, roughly one alpha in eight thousand came back at large angles, thousands of times too many for any smeared-out atom to explain: a single violent deflection, off something tiny, heavy, and charged. The atom’s mass and positive charge had to be packed into a nucleus a hundred thousand times smaller than the atom itself. Rutherford later called it the most incredible event of his life, “as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.” The method they invented, firing a beam at a target and reading the structure of the target from the angles things scatter to, is still how we interrogate matter today, from electron microscopes to the Large Hadron Collider. Here you repeat the discovery with your own hands and test whether the scattering really follows Rutherford’s famous prediction.

What you will measure: the rate of alpha particles scattered from a metal foil as a function of angle, testing the characteristic 1/sin⁴(θ/2) dependence that signals a pointlike, charged nucleus.

Compton Scattering

Is light a wave or a particle? By 1923 the photon idea was still contested, and Compton’s experiment settled it: X-rays scattering off electrons shift in wavelength exactly as if a particle of light had struck a particle of matter in a billiard-ball collision. The result won Compton the 1927 Nobel Prize and made the photon undeniable. That shift, tiny for visible light but large for gamma rays, is now a workhorse of physics: it sets how gamma rays deposit energy in detectors and in people, and it powers instruments from gamma-ray telescopes to medical imagers. In this experiment you play the collision out at a set of angles and check the photon picture quantitatively.

It’s also a process you will never escape in this course: Compton scattering is the dominant fate of a gamma ray at these energies, so the continuum and edge it stamps onto every spectrum you take all semester are this experiment running quietly in the background of every other one. And astronomers run it in reverse: in inverse Compton scattering, photons steal energy from fast electrons, which is how some of the most violent objects in the sky, jets from black holes among them, broadcast at the highest energies we can see.

What you will measure: the energy of gamma rays scattered from a target as a function of scattering angle, testing the Compton formula and extracting the rest-mass energy of the electron from your own data.

Timing and coincidence

Speed of Light

The speed of light is the speed limit of the universe, the conversion factor between space and time in relativity, and since 1983 the quantity that defines the meter. (A pleasing consequence: c can no longer be measured “wrong”; any disagreement with the accepted value is now, by definition, a measurement of your apparatus, which is exactly the spirit of this lab.) The chase for the number is old and honorable, opening in 1676 with Ole Rømer noticing that Jupiter’s moons ran late whenever the Earth was far away. Measuring it directly is a classic challenge: light crosses a lab bench in a few nanoseconds, so the experiment is really about mastering fast timing, the same craft that underlies GPS, particle physics triggers, and high-speed communication. Albert Michelson spent five decades refining this measurement, and the precision instruments he built for it made him the first American to win a science Nobel Prize (1907). Your light source is elegant in itself: positrons annihilating with electrons, sending two gamma rays flying apart at exactly the speed you are trying to measure.

What you will measure: the flight time of gamma rays over measured distances, giving you the speed of light. You will confirm the accepted value and quantify what limits the precision of your setup.

Cosmic Rays

The Earth is under constant bombardment by particles with energies far beyond any accelerator, and their arrival at ground level, mostly as muons, is a free beam of relativistic particles crossing your lab right now. Cosmic rays are where particle physics began: Victor Hess discovered them in 1912 by carrying electrometers up in balloon flights (a Nobel Prize in 1936, shared with Carl Anderson, whose positron also turned up in cosmic rays), and the positron, the muon, and the pion were all first seen in them. Detecting them teaches the coincidence technique: single detectors fire constantly on noise, but demanding that several fire simultaneously filters out everything except a real particle punching through all of them. That trick is the foundation of every modern particle physics experiment.

The muons themselves have lately become a tool in their own right. Muography images things far too big to X-ray, using the muons as the penetrating rays: count how many arrive through an object from each direction, and a deficit means denser or thicker material along that line, a radiograph with muons instead of X-rays. In 2017 it revealed a previously unknown hidden chamber inside the Great Pyramid of Giza. The paddle-and-coincidence instrument you master here is the same species of device, pointed at the sky.

What you will measure: coincidence rates in a stack of scintillator paddles, the detection efficiency of each paddle, and the muon rate as a function of zenith angle, testing the characteristic cos²θ distribution of atmospheric muons.

Muon Lifetime

Every second, muons created by cosmic rays in the upper atmosphere rain down through you. Classically they shouldn’t make it: they live about two microseconds, far too short to survive the trip, and the fact that they arrive anyway is time dilation, special relativity operating overhead all day, every day. The lifetime itself is a fundamental number: the muon decays by the weak interaction, the theory Enrico Fermi invented for beta decay (part of the body of work behind his 1938 Nobel Prize), and the muon lifetime gives one of the cleanest measurements of that force’s strength, the Fermi constant. The muon itself was so unexpected when it appeared in cosmic rays that I. I. Rabi famously asked, “Who ordered that?” Nobody has answered him yet: the muon is a heavier copy of the electron, one of three “generations” of matter whose existence remains one of physics’ open questions, and precision measurements of its magnetic moment (the Fermilab muon g-2 experiment) have spent the past decade probing whether it feels forces we haven’t discovered. In this experiment muons stop and die inside your detector, and you clock each one.

What you will measure: the distribution of time delays between a muon stopping in the scintillator and its decay, an exponential whose time constant is the muon lifetime. You will confirm the accepted value of about 2.2 microseconds and quantify the limitations of your measurement.

Nuclear Lifetime

An excited nucleus does not drop to its ground state on a schedule; it decays at a random moment, and the randomness is absolute. A quantum state carries no internal clock: two identical nuclei excited at the same instant decay at different, individually unpredictable times, and a nucleus that has already survived ten lifetimes is exactly as likely to decay in the next nanosecond as one excited a moment ago. Nothing inside it ages. That memorylessness is what forces the decay curve to be an exponential, and this experiment makes it unusually visible, because the delayed coincidence method times individual nuclei: catch the gamma ray that announces one nucleus entering its excited state, clock how long that single quantum system chooses to live, and watch thousands of such private decisions assemble into the exponential.

The number you extract is quantum information twice over. First, through the time-energy uncertainty principle, a state that lives about 100 nanoseconds cannot have a perfectly sharp energy: its intrinsic width is ħ/τ, a few billionths of an eV on a 14 keV transition, an energy defined to a part in 10¹³. Measuring the lifetime is measuring one side of an uncertainty relation; the other side, that absurdly narrow linewidth, is what makes this same iron-57 state the workhorse of Mössbauer spectroscopy (a Nobel Prize in 1961). Second, the lifetime’s value is a structure probe: the decay rate depends on how well the initial and final nuclear states overlap, so transitions where many nucleons move collectively run much faster than the lone-nucleon benchmark, while hindered rearrangements run slower by enormous factors, the same physics that stretches nuclear lifetimes from femtoseconds to, for some isomers, centuries. A lifetime is not a mere timestamp; it is a report on what the nucleons inside are doing.

What you will measure: the distribution of delays between the two gamma rays of a nuclear cascade, an exponential decay curve whose time constant is the lifetime of the intermediate state, roughly 100 nanoseconds.

Gamma-Gamma Angular Correlation challenge lab

How do we know the spin of a nucleus, a quantum property with no classical handle to grab? One of the cleanest answers: when a nucleus emits two gamma rays in quick succession, the directions of the two are correlated, and the shape of that correlation is dictated by the angular momenta of the states involved. Measuring coincidence rate versus angle reads quantum numbers directly out of geometry. This technique built much of our map of nuclear structure, and its most famous descendant changed physics: Chien-Shiung Wu’s 1956 measurement of decay products’ directions from aligned nuclei, an angular-correlation experiment at heart, proved that nature distinguishes left from right (parity violation), earning Lee and Yang the Nobel Prize the following year. The method still earns its keep in materials science today: perturbed angular correlation watches how the magnetic and electric fields inside a solid twist the correlation pattern, turning a single radioactive atom into an embedded reporter on its crystal neighborhood.

What you will measure: the rate of coincident gamma-ray pairs from a cascade decay as a function of the angle between two detectors, testing the predicted correlation function W(θ) and comparing its anisotropy with the value expected for the known spin sequence.

ACAR (Angular Correlation of Annihilation Radiation)

The positron was the first antimatter ever found: predicted by Dirac’s equation (Nobel Prize, 1933) and discovered in cosmic rays by Carl Anderson (Nobel Prize, 1936). When a positron meets an electron, the pair can even pause on the way out as positronium, a fleeting hydrogen-like atom with no nucleus at all, before annihilating into two gamma rays flying apart back-to-back. But only exactly back-to-back if the pair was at rest: any electron momentum tips the two photons slightly off 180 degrees. That tiny deviation turns annihilation into a probe of the electrons inside matter, sharp enough to map the Fermi surface, the momentum-space boundary that governs how metals conduct. The same physics runs the other direction in medicine: positron emission tomography (PET) scanners locate tumors by tracing annihilation photon pairs back to their origin.

What you will measure: the coincidence rate of annihilation photon pairs as a function of the small angle away from 180 degrees, whose distribution reflects the momentum distribution of electrons in the sample.

X-rays, materials and quantum

XRD (X-ray Diffraction)

Most solid matter is crystalline, endless repeating lattices of atoms, and the spacing of those lattices happens to match the wavelength of X-rays. Send X-rays into a crystal and they interfere, emerging only at angles satisfying Bragg’s law, so the diffraction pattern is a direct readout of atomic architecture. This one technique identified the structure of table salt, penicillin, and DNA, and it remains the daily workhorse of materials science, geology, and drug development: hand a lab an unknown powder, and diffraction names the crystal. Few methods have collected more Nobel Prizes: von Laue for discovering the diffraction itself (1914), the Braggs, father and son, for using it to solve structures (1915, with Lawrence Bragg still the youngest science laureate at 25), and the DNA double helix (1962) rested on Rosalind Franklin’s diffraction patterns.

What you will measure: diffraction patterns of crystalline powder samples, from which you extract lattice spacings via Bragg’s law, determine each crystal’s structure and lattice constant, and identify unknowns.

XRF (X-ray Fluorescence)

Knock an inner electron out of an atom and the atom answers with an X-ray whose energy is unique to its element, a fingerprint set by nothing but the nuclear charge. That regularity, Moseley’s law, is why the periodic table is ordered by atomic number, and Moseley found it just before dying at Gallipoli at age 27; it is widely held that only his death kept the discovery from a Nobel Prize, and the X-ray spectroscopy it founded earned one for Manne Siegbahn in 1924. X-ray fluorescence turns the fingerprint into a practical superpower: museums use it to unmask forged paintings, geologists to assay ore, and regulators to find lead in toys, all without damaging the sample. It even does field work off-planet: the Mars rovers carry X-ray spectrometers that read Martian rocks element by element, the same physics you will point at unknowns here, running on another world.

What you will measure: the characteristic X-ray line energies of a range of elements, building your own Moseley plot from their systematics, and the elemental composition of unknown samples.

SQUID (Superconducting Quantum Interference Device)

Quantum mechanics is usually the physics of the very small, but in a superconductor billions of electrons condense into a single quantum state you can wire into a circuit. Two consequences follow: current flows through an insulating gap with no voltage (the Josephson effect, predicted by Brian Josephson as a 22-year-old graduate student, a Nobel Prize in 1973), and magnetic flux through a superconducting loop is quantized in units of h/2e. A SQUID combines both into the most sensitive magnetometer ever built, able to detect the magnetic whisper of a human brain. And the Josephson junction has a second act underway: it is the building block of superconducting qubits, so the device you tune on this bench runs on the same physics at the heart of today’s quantum computers. In this lab, quantum mechanics stops being microscopic: interference effects show up directly on your instruments.

What you will measure: the current-voltage curve of a Josephson junction, and the periodic voltage modulation of the SQUID with applied magnetic flux, whose period is one flux quantum, giving you a measurement of h/2e.

Saturation Spectroscopy challenge lab

Atomic spectral lines carry exquisite information, including hyperfine structure, the tiny splittings from the interaction between the nucleus and its electrons. The catch: in a room-temperature gas, atoms rush in all directions and the Doppler effect smears the lines, burying the detail. Saturation spectroscopy is the elegant escape: two laser beams travel through the gas in opposite directions, and only atoms standing still (as seen along the beam) can talk to both at once, so the Doppler blur drops away. This Doppler-free trick is a foundation of laser cooling and the atomic clocks that keep GPS honest, and the physicists who developed laser spectroscopy to this precision collected Nobel Prizes for it twice: Bloembergen and Schawlow in 1981, and Hänsch and Hall in 2005 for pushing it to measurements of dizzying exactness. Hyperfine structure, the thing you will resolve, is not a spectroscopic curiosity but infrastructure: the SI second itself is defined as exactly 9,192,631,770 oscillations of a hyperfine transition in cesium, so splittings like these literally keep the world’s time.

What you will measure: the Doppler-free absorption spectrum of rubidium, resolving hyperfine transitions hidden inside the Doppler-broadened line and measuring their splittings against accepted values.

NMR (Nuclear Magnetic Resonance) challenge lab

A proton is a tiny magnet, and in a magnetic field it precesses like a spinning top, at a frequency exactly proportional to the field. Nuclear magnetic resonance is the art of tipping those spins with a radio-frequency pulse and listening to them ring. From this one phenomenon grew two revolutions: NMR spectroscopy, which lets chemists read molecular structure from frequency shifts, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which maps the water in your body without ionizing radiation. The trail of Nobel Prizes runs through the whole story: Rabi for the resonance method (1944), Bloch and Purcell for NMR in ordinary matter (1952), and Lauterbur and Mansfield for turning it into MRI (2003). The heart of both is what you study here: resonance, precession, and how spins relax back to equilibrium.

What you will measure: the resonance frequency of protons in a known magnetic field, giving the proton’s gyromagnetic ratio, and the relaxation times that characterize how the spins return to equilibrium, the same quantities that generate contrast in an MRI image.
