Welcome

The objective of this course is to become familiar with the equipment and techniques of experimental physics. Many of the experiments center on radiation detectors and the electronics that process their signals (timing and energy), while others are built around entirely different apparatus, each designed specifically for the phenomenon being investigated. Across all of them you will develop the common toolkit of the experimentalist: data acquisition, statistical analysis and curve fitting, and the communication of results in the forms physicists actually use, journal-style reports, posters, and talks. Most of the experiments in this lab replicate breakthrough measurements that earned (or famously deserved) Nobel Prizes; you will reproduce them with research-grade equipment.

Energy spectroscopy

Alpha Spectroscopy

When a charged particle passes through matter, it loses energy through thousands of small collisions with the electrons of the material. The rate of that energy loss, called the stopping power, is one of the most consequential quantities in applied physics: it determines how thick a spacecraft’s shielding needs to be, how deep a proton-therapy beam penetrates before depositing its dose, and how particle detectors distinguish one particle species from another. In this experiment we send alpha particles through thin metal foils inside a vacuum chamber and measure how much energy they lose, allowing you to directly measure the stopping power that Bohr and Bethe first worked out a century ago. The sharp rise in energy loss just before a charged particle stops is called the Bragg peak, named for William Henry Bragg, who mapped alpha stopping in the early 1900s. This peak is the reason charged-particle therapy works, since a proton beam can be tuned to deposit most of its dose at the depth of a tumor and very little anywhere else. The newest version of this idea, targeted alpha therapy, attaches alpha emitters to tumor-seeking molecules; because the stopping power of an alpha is so large, its entire dose is delivered within a few cell diameters of the decay, killing the targeted cancer cell while sparing the surrounding tissue.

The underlying physics is also satisfying to understand. A slower particle spends more time near each electron it passes, so the slower an alpha gets, the faster it loses energy (the stopping power grows roughly as 1/v²), until it deposits everything in a short final burst. This is the origin of the Bragg peak. The same relationship between energy loss and speed is how modern detectors, from collider experiments to space instruments, identify particle species, making this lab a first lesson in particle identification.

What you will measure: the energy spectrum of an alpha source, and the energy lost in foils of different metals and thicknesses, giving you the stopping power dE/dx of each material and a comparison against theory.

Gamma Spectroscopy

Every radioactive isotope emits gamma rays at a specific set of energies, and this set acts as a fingerprint that uniquely identifies the isotope. Reading these fingerprints is how we monitor reactors, screen cargo for nuclear material, date archaeological finds, and identify newly synthesized elements. In this experiment we use a high-purity germanium (HPGe) detector, a single crystal of some of the purest material ever produced, with an energy resolution sharp enough to separate lines that other detectors blur together.

That resolution also gives us direct access to nuclear structure. Some heavy nuclei are permanently deformed and can rotate, and their gamma spectra contain rotational bands whose spacing tells us the moment of inertia of the rotating nucleus. Being able to measure that moment of inertia then reveals new and confounding physics by allowing us to explore what kind of object a spinning nucleus actually is. A rigid body predicts one moment of inertia and a rotating fluid predicts another, and the measured value falls between the two. The explanation is that the paired nucleons behave something like a superfluid, rotating only partially along with the nucleus. The extremely fine energy resolution of germanium allows you to directly measure this behavior, and explaining why the nucleus appears to be neither a solid nor a liquid earned Aage Bohr, Ben Mottelson, and James Rainwater the 1975 Nobel Prize in Physics.

What you will measure: calibrated gamma-ray spectra from known and unknown sources, the energy resolution of your detector, and the rotational band of a deformed nucleus, from which you extract its moment of inertia and test it against the rigid-body and fluid pictures, expecting it to match neither.

Rutherford Scattering

In 1911 this experiment rewrote our picture of the atom. The accepted model at the time was J.J. Thomson’s “plum pudding,” in which electrons are embedded in a diffuse ball of positive charge, with the atom’s mass and charge spread out smoothly. In such an atom an alpha particle has nothing solid to hit, so it should pass through the thousands of atomic layers in a gold foil while being deflected by only tiny fractions of a degree, and even the accumulated effect of many such deflections predicts essentially no scattering at large angles. This is what Rutherford, already a Nobel laureate for his radioactivity work, expected when he set Hans Geiger and a twenty-year-old undergraduate, Ernest Marsden, to watch for scattered alphas. Instead, roughly one alpha in eight thousand scattered to large angles, thousands of times more than any smeared-out atom could explain. The only consistent interpretation was a single violent deflection from something small, heavy, and charged, meaning the atom’s mass and positive charge must be concentrated in a nucleus roughly a hundred thousand times smaller than the atom itself. Rutherford later called it the most incredible event of his life, “as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.” The method itself, firing a beam at a target and reconstructing the structure of the target from the scattering angles, is still how we investigate matter today, from electron microscopes to the Large Hadron Collider. In this experiment you repeat the original measurement and test whether the scattering follows Rutherford’s famous prediction.

What you will measure: the rate of alpha particles scattered from a metal foil as a function of angle, testing the characteristic 1/sin⁴(θ/2) dependence that signals a pointlike, charged nucleus.

Compton Scattering

By 1923 physicists were still divided over whether light is a wave or a particle, and Compton’s experiment settled the question: X-rays scattering off electrons shift in wavelength exactly as they would if a particle of light collided with a particle of matter in a simple two-body collision. The result won Compton the 1927 Nobel Prize and made the photon impossible to dismiss. The wavelength shift, tiny for visible light but substantial for gamma rays, is now a workhorse of physics, governing how gamma rays deposit energy in detectors and in people, and underlying instruments from gamma-ray telescopes to medical imagers. In this experiment you measure the energy of the scattered photons across a range of angles, allowing you to test the Compton formula quantitatively and to extract the rest-mass energy of the electron from your own data.

Compton scattering is also a process you will encounter all semester, whichever experiments you choose, because it is the dominant interaction for gamma rays at these energies; the continuum and edge it produces appear in every gamma spectrum taken in this course. Astronomers make use of the reverse process as well. In inverse Compton scattering, photons gain energy from fast electrons, and this is how some of the most energetic objects in the sky, including the jets launched by black holes, produce the highest-energy radiation we observe.

What you will measure: the energy of gamma rays scattered from a target as a function of scattering angle, testing the Compton formula and extracting the rest-mass energy of the electron from your own data.

Timing and coincidence

Speed of Light

The speed of light is the speed limit of the universe, the conversion factor between space and time in relativity, and, since 1983, the quantity that defines the meter. None of this was obvious at the start. For most of history it was an open question whether light traveled at a finite speed at all; Galileo tried to time it with lanterns on distant hilltops and could only conclude that it was too fast for him to measure. The first real determination came in 1676, when Ole Rømer noticed that the moons of Jupiter ran late whenever the Earth was farther away, with the delay revealing the extra time their light needed to reach us. By Albert Michelson’s era the value itself was known, and the reason he kept pushing the precision for five decades is telling: the sharpest available test of whether the Earth moves through a hypothetical “luminiferous aether” was to compare light’s travel along different directions, and his interferometer finding no difference at all, to remarkable precision, helped set the stage for special relativity. The instruments he built in that pursuit made him the first American to win a science Nobel Prize (1907). Measuring c directly remains a genuine challenge, since light crosses a lab bench in a few nanoseconds, making this experiment an exercise in mastering fast timing, the same techniques that underlie GPS, particle-physics triggers, and high-speed communication. Our light source is a fitting one: positrons annihilating with electrons, sending pairs of gamma rays flying apart at exactly the speed you are trying to measure.

What you will measure: the flight time of gamma rays over measured distances, giving you the speed of light. You will confirm the accepted value and quantify what limits the precision of your setup.

Cosmic Rays

The Earth is under constant bombardment by particles with energies far beyond anything our accelerators can produce, and their arrival at ground level, mostly as muons, provides a free beam of relativistic particles crossing your lab right now. Cosmic rays are where particle physics began. Victor Hess discovered them in 1912 by carrying electrometers up in balloon flights, work recognized by the 1936 Nobel Prize (shared with Carl Anderson, whose positron discovery also came from cosmic rays), and the positron, the muon, and the pion were all first observed in them. Detecting cosmic rays also teaches the coincidence technique: individual detectors fire constantly on noise, but requiring several detectors to fire simultaneously filters out everything except a real particle passing through all of them. This technique is the foundation of every modern particle-physics experiment.

The muons themselves have recently become a measurement tool in their own right. Muography images objects far too large to X-ray by using the muons as the penetrating rays: counting how many arrive through an object from each direction reveals where the dense material is, producing a radiograph with muons instead of X-rays. In 2017 this technique revealed a previously unknown chamber inside the Great Pyramid of Giza, using the same kind of paddle-and-coincidence instrument you will master here, pointed at the sky.

What you will measure: coincidence rates in a stack of scintillator paddles, the detection efficiency of each paddle, and the muon rate as a function of zenith angle, testing the characteristic cos²θ distribution of atmospheric muons.

Muon Lifetime

Every second, muons created by cosmic rays in the upper atmosphere rain down through you. Classically they should not survive the trip, since they live about two microseconds, far too short to cover the distance, and the fact that they arrive anyway is a direct consequence of time dilation, with special relativity operating overhead all day, every day. The lifetime itself is a fundamental quantity. The muon decays through the weak interaction, the force Enrico Fermi first described in his theory of beta decay (part of the work recognized by his 1938 Nobel Prize), and measuring the muon lifetime provides one of the cleanest determinations of that force’s strength, the Fermi constant. The muon was so unexpected when it appeared in cosmic rays that I. I. Rabi famously asked, “Who ordered that?” The question still stands. The muon is a heavier copy of the electron, one of three “generations” of matter whose existence remains an open question in physics, and precision measurements of its magnetic moment at the Fermilab muon g-2 experiment have spent the past decade testing whether it feels forces we have not yet discovered. In this experiment, muons stop and decay inside your detector, and you measure the time each one takes.

What you will measure: the distribution of time delays between a muon stopping in the scintillator and its decay, an exponential whose time constant is the muon lifetime. You will confirm the accepted value of about 2.2 microseconds and quantify the limitations of your measurement.

Nuclear Lifetime

An excited nuclear state does not decay on a schedule. Each nucleus decays at a random moment, and this randomness is a fundamental property of quantum states: two identical nuclei excited at the same instant will decay at different, unpredictable times, and a nucleus that has already survived ten lifetimes is exactly as likely to decay in the next nanosecond as one that was just excited. There is no internal clock keeping track. This memoryless behavior is what forces the population to decay exponentially, and the delayed-coincidence technique used in this experiment is how we observe it directly. We time individual nuclei by catching the gamma ray emitted when a nucleus enters its excited state and measuring how long it takes for a second gamma ray to announce that it left. The resulting exponential decay curve gets populated one decay at a time, eventually revealing the characteristic decay time of that excited state.

The lifetime you extract carries quantum information in two separate ways. First, through the time-energy uncertainty principle, a state that lives about 100 nanoseconds cannot have a perfectly defined energy: its intrinsic width is ħ/τ, a few billionths of an eV on a 14 keV transition, or an energy defined to roughly one part in 10¹³. Measuring the lifetime is therefore a measurement of one side of an uncertainty relation, and that remarkably narrow linewidth is what makes this same iron-57 state the workhorse of Mössbauer spectroscopy (recognized with the 1961 Nobel Prize). Second, the value of the lifetime is itself a probe of nuclear structure, because the decay rate depends on how well the initial and final nuclear states overlap: transitions in which many nucleons move collectively run much faster than the single-nucleon benchmark, while hindered rearrangements can be slower by enormous factors, the same physics that stretches nuclear lifetimes from femtoseconds to centuries for some isomers. In this way, a measured lifetime is more than a timestamp, serving as a direct report on what the nucleons inside are doing.

What you will measure: the distribution of delays between the two gamma rays of a nuclear cascade, an exponential decay curve whose time constant is the lifetime of the intermediate state, roughly 100 nanoseconds.

Gamma-Gamma Angular Correlation challenge lab

Determining the spin of a nucleus is a real experimental puzzle, since spin is a quantum property with no classical handle we can grab. One of the cleanest solutions uses the light the nucleus emits. When a nucleus emits two gamma rays in quick succession, their directions are correlated, and the shape of that correlation is set by the angular momenta of the nuclear states involved, so measuring the coincidence rate as a function of the angle between two detectors allows us to read quantum numbers directly from geometry. This technique built much of our map of nuclear structure, and its most famous descendant changed physics. Chien-Shiung Wu’s 1956 measurement of the emission directions from aligned nuclei, an angular-correlation experiment at its heart, proved that nature distinguishes left from right, a result known as parity violation that earned Lee and Yang the Nobel Prize the following year. The method also remains useful in materials science, where perturbed angular correlation measures how the magnetic and electric fields inside a solid modify the correlation pattern, turning a single radioactive probe atom into a reporter on its local environment in the crystal.

What you will measure: the rate of coincident gamma-ray pairs from a cascade decay as a function of the angle between two detectors, testing the predicted correlation function W(θ) and comparing its anisotropy with the value expected for the known spin sequence.

ACAR (Angular Correlation of Annihilation Radiation)

The positron was the first antimatter ever observed, predicted by Dirac’s equation (a Nobel Prize in 1933) and discovered in cosmic rays by Carl Anderson (a Nobel Prize in 1936). When a positron meets an electron, the pair can briefly form positronium, a hydrogen-like atom with no nucleus, before annihilating into two gamma rays that fly apart back-to-back. The two photons are only exactly back-to-back if the annihilating pair was at rest, so any momentum carried by the electron tips the pair slightly away from 180 degrees. Being able to measure that small deviation turns annihilation into a probe of the electrons inside matter, precise enough to map the Fermi surface, the boundary in momentum space that governs how metals conduct electricity. The same physics runs in the other direction in medicine, where positron emission tomography (PET) scanners locate tumors by tracing annihilation photon pairs back to their origin.

What you will measure: the coincidence rate of annihilation photon pairs as a function of the small angle away from 180 degrees, whose distribution reflects the momentum distribution of electrons in the sample.

X-rays, materials and quantum

XRD (X-ray Diffraction)

Most solid matter is crystalline, made of endlessly repeating lattices of atoms, and the spacing of those lattices happens to match the wavelength of X-rays. When X-rays pass through a crystal they interfere, emerging only at the specific angles that satisfy Bragg’s law, which makes the diffraction pattern a direct readout of the atomic architecture. This single technique identified the structures of table salt, penicillin, and DNA, and it remains the daily workhorse of materials science, geology, and drug development: given an unknown powder, diffraction can name the crystal. Few methods have collected more Nobel Prizes. Von Laue won in 1914 for discovering the diffraction itself; the Braggs, father and son, won in 1915 for using it to solve structures (with Lawrence Bragg, at 25, still the youngest science laureate); and the 1962 prize for the DNA double helix rested on Rosalind Franklin’s diffraction patterns.

What you will measure: diffraction patterns of crystalline powder samples, from which you extract lattice spacings via Bragg’s law, determine each crystal’s structure and lattice constant, and identify unknowns.

XRF (X-ray Fluorescence)

When an inner electron is knocked out of an atom, the atom responds by emitting an X-ray whose energy is unique to that element, a fingerprint set by nothing but the nuclear charge. This regularity, known as Moseley’s law, is the reason the periodic table is ordered by atomic number. Moseley established it just before dying at Gallipoli at age 27, and it is widely held that only his death kept the discovery from a Nobel Prize; the X-ray spectroscopy it founded did earn one for Manne Siegbahn in 1924. X-ray fluorescence (XRF) turns this fingerprint into a remarkably practical tool: museums use it to identify forged paintings, geologists use it to assay ore, and regulators use it to find lead in toys, all without damaging the sample. The technique even works off-planet, with the Mars rovers carrying X-ray spectrometers that read Martian rocks element by element using the same physics you will apply to unknown samples here.

What you will measure: the characteristic X-ray line energies of a range of elements, building your own Moseley plot from their systematics, and the elemental composition of unknown samples.

SQUID (Superconducting Quantum Interference Device)

Quantum mechanics is usually the physics of the very small, but in a superconductor billions of electrons condense into a single quantum state that can be wired into a circuit. Two consequences follow. Current can flow through an insulating gap with no voltage applied, an effect predicted by Brian Josephson as a 22-year-old graduate student (a Nobel Prize in 1973), and the magnetic flux through a superconducting loop is quantized in units of h/2e. A SQUID combines both effects into the most sensitive magnetometer ever built, sensitive enough to detect the magnetic fields produced by the human brain. The Josephson junction also has a second act underway as the building block of superconducting qubits, meaning the device you tune on this bench runs on the same physics at the heart of today’s quantum computers. In this lab, quantum mechanics stops being microscopic, with interference effects showing up directly on your instruments.

What you will measure: the current-voltage curve of a Josephson junction, and the periodic voltage modulation of the SQUID with applied magnetic flux, whose period is one flux quantum, giving you a measurement of h/2e.

NMR (Nuclear Magnetic Resonance) challenge lab

A proton is a tiny magnet, and in a magnetic field it precesses like a spinning top at a frequency exactly proportional to the field. This is the resonance that I. I. Rabi first learned to drive with radio waves (a Nobel Prize in 1944). The measurement works as follows: a radio-frequency pulse at the precession frequency tips the nuclear spins away from the field direction, and because the tipped spins precess in step, their combined magnetization sweeps past a pickup coil and induces an oscillating voltage at exactly the precession frequency. That signal fades as the spins fall out of step and settle back to equilibrium, and both the frequency and the fading carry physics. Bloch and Purcell showed independently in 1946 that this works in ordinary solids and liquids, not just atomic beams (the 1952 Nobel Prize), and from their tabletop effect grew two revolutions: NMR spectroscopy, which lets chemists read molecular structure from small frequency shifts, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which maps the water in your body without ionizing radiation and earned Lauterbur and Mansfield the 2003 prize. The heart of all of it is what you study here: resonance, precession, and how spins relax back to equilibrium.

What you will measure: the resonance frequency of protons in a known magnetic field, giving the proton’s gyromagnetic ratio, and the relaxation times that characterize how the spins return to equilibrium, the same quantities that generate contrast in an MRI image.

Saturation Spectroscopy challenge lab

Atomic spectral lines carry exquisite information, including hyperfine structure, the tiny splittings produced by the interaction between the nucleus and its electrons. The difficulty is that in a room-temperature gas, atoms move in all directions and the Doppler effect smears the lines, burying the detail. Saturation spectroscopy provides an elegant way around this problem: two laser beams travel through the gas in opposite directions, and only atoms that are standing still (as seen along the beam) can interact with both beams at once, so the Doppler blur drops away. This Doppler-free technique is a foundation of laser cooling and of the atomic clocks that keep GPS honest, and the physicists who developed laser spectroscopy to this precision collected Nobel Prizes for it twice, Bloembergen and Schawlow in 1981, and Hänsch and Hall in 2005.

The splittings themselves reach well beyond this bench. Hyperfine structure exists because the nucleus is itself a tiny magnet interacting with its electrons, so these splittings are how atomic physicists first determined nuclear spins and magnetic moments without ever touching a nucleus. The most famous hyperfine transition is the 21 cm line of hydrogen, the workhorse of radio astronomy: it maps our galaxy’s spiral arms, feeds the rotation curves that pointed to dark matter, and was chosen for the Voyager record as the unit of length and time another physicist should recognize. Closer to home, the SI second is defined as exactly 9,192,631,770 oscillations of a cesium hyperfine transition, so every clock on Earth ultimately traces back to a splitting of the same kind you will measure. The current frontier runs through the same physics as well, with trapped-ion and neutral-atom quantum computers storing their qubits in hyperfine states, which are prized for how long they maintain quantum coherence.

What you will measure: the Doppler-free absorption spectrum of rubidium, resolving hyperfine transitions hidden inside the Doppler-broadened line and measuring their splittings against accepted values.
